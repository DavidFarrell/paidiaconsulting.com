AI before the buzz

AI learns patterns.

  • AI uses previous data to predict values›
  • Think of it as fitting a curve to old data, then using the curve to make predictions›
  • So it can only ever recreate an approximate version of the data it has seen›
How AI learns

How AI fits the curve.

  • It doesn't guess: it calculates which direction would reduce the error›
  • Each step nudges every number a tiny amount towards a better fit›
  • Repeat thousands of times and the curve settles into the shape of the data›
  • This is gradient descent: the engine behind every model you've heard of›
How AI learns

Recommendation systems.

  • Suggest things to people, based on what they know about you and about people like you›
  • Think Netflix and Spotify›
  • If people like you click a link, it learns that's a good link to show you›
How AI learns

You meet the quirks quickly.

  • If your data is poisoned, the outputs lose value›
  • AI only knows what it has seen›
How AI learns

The design of a system sets its limits.

  • You need to understand these limits when you use AI at work›
  • This system outputs a likelihood of Covid-19 or pneumonia from a chest X-ray›
  • But it cannot output anything else›
  • So what happens if someone submits an X-ray of healthy lungs?›
Limitations

Generative AI.

From predicting numbers to making new stuff.

Generative AI creates new content.

  • It learns from huge datasets and creates new ‘stuff’ that fits the patterns it learned›
  • It has been shown the relationship between text and an output, so you can steer it with words›
  • But if it hasn't seen something like what you ask for, it can't do it›
Generative AI

It recreates the biases in its data.

  • It makes content ‘like’ the data it has seen›
  • Some biases are innocuous›
  • Some reinforce stereotypes and can be really offensive›
  • All AI systems reflect the data they were trained on. Generative AI is no different›
Generative AI

As AI improves, bias gets harder to spot.

  • The obvious gaps get patched›
  • But they are still there. Be wary of this risk›
  • Ultimately, you are responsible for your use of AI, so be defensive›

Uses of GenAI

Language models.

A deep dive into ChatGPT, Claude and Copilot.

They learn what word comes next.

  • Trained on almost unimaginable amounts of text (e.g. all of the internet)›
  • They learn ‘curves’ that predict where a sentence goes next›
  • With a complicated enough curve, you could model Shakespeare›
  • When you prompt, you (kind of) guide it through the dictionary from what you've said so far›
Language models

AIs are guided by context.

  • Your prompt is context that shapes where the curve goes›
  • How it responds depends on the data it was trained on›
  • “I'm thinking of a number between 1 and 10...”›
Language models

Easily guided. By us, and by bad actors.

  • AIs are easily steered by prompts: we can get them to do what we want›
  • And bad actors will get them to do what they want›
Language models

The skill that matters is ours.

How to get the giraffe, not the snake.

AI is now good enough that the limit is usually us.

  • Give today's tools a well-defined task and enough context, and they produce genuinely useful work›
  • True of all the big ones: ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and Copilot›
  • They differ in the details, but the skill of using them carries across›
  • And they keep getting better, faster than our habits change›
Using it well

Tell it how hard to think.

  • Every AI tool lets you choose how long it thinks before it answers›
  • If you don't choose, it decides for you›
  • For anything complicated (analysis, drafting, building something), ask it to think deeper›
  • In Copilot: the menu at the top of the chat. Choose Think deeper›
Using it well

Three skills, in any of these tools.

Context

Tell it the situation: who it's for, what has gone wrong before, what you already know. Paste in your material.

Specificity

Say exactly what you want and what good looks like: the format, the audience, the constraints, the test of success.

Iteration

Treat the first answer as a draft. Look hard at it, say what's wrong, and go again.

Using it well

Ask for a balloon animal and you get a snake.

  • Generic input, generic output›
  • Twist the back legs, the forelegs, leave a long neck: a giraffe›
  • Context and specifics are the twists›
  • Your job is to opinionate it. Lazy use, aka slop, won't be acceptable at work›
Using it well

Ask Copilot for a service, and you get a snake.

THE PROMPT
“Create a prototype website for a council service where residents can request a replacement for a street tree that is being removed. Go ahead and show me.”
  • It works, it looks fine, and it asks sensible-looking questions›
  • It lets you pick any kind of tree, even ones that aren't suitable›
  • It confirms with a reference, then says nothing about what happens next›
Using it well

Meanwhile, what that kind of service did to a real person.

  • A notice on his dying street tree: it's coming down, request a new one at this web address›
  • He can't request a new tree until the stump is removed›
  • Stump removal: a different department, a different website, and only once the tree is down›
  • The new-tree form asks things the city already knows, then offers every tree on earth›
  • Sequoia: rejected a week later. Oak: rejected. Cypress: rejected. Then they concreted the spot›

He never got a tree.

Using it well

And you get a service: the giraffe.

  • Starts from the notice on the tree. No questions the council can already answer›
  • Removal, stump and planting as one timeline, with dates›
  • Only trees that fit this street, and why there's no sequoia›
  • The bad outcome handled: planted nearby, never just concrete›

Every feature traces back to a problem in the user's story.

Using it well

Then iterate: check its work as you would a new colleague's.

What it still got wrong
  • The GOV.UK crown and name. A council service needs the council's own brand›
  • The “you are here” marker sits on a step that hasn't happened yet›
  • Tree heights and cable clearances are its own guesses. In real life the tree officer confirms them›
  • So you tell it, and go again. Spotting these is your job, not the AI's›
Using it well

Context beats brevity.

  • A short, tight prompt can be perfect›
  • A long, rambling prompt is also fine, if it carries context›
  • “Did I give it enough information?” beats “Did I phrase it tightly?”›
Using it well

Why I dictate.

  • Crafting a good sentence is hard work›
  • Talking is fast: stream your raw thoughts, no editing tax. You give it far more context›
  • Voice input matters for accessibility, not just speed›
  • There's a microphone in Copilot, ChatGPT and Claude, and dictation built into Windows and Mac›

So what are language models good at?

  • They can do a lot, and much of the value is in knowing what they can do for you›

What AI is good at

Language manipulation.

  • Turn rough bullet points into a polite email›
  • “Get rid of any passive aggressiveness and make it nice, please”›
What AI is good at

Adapting text for different audiences.

  • It takes away the effort of tailoring a message to its reader›
  • “Rewrite it for my boss, and don't get me fired”›
  • “Simplify the language”›
  • “Translate to German” - though if it's important, you'd want to check it was good›
  • But we should keep a human in the loop for important things›
What AI is good at

Reading long documents.

  • As language processors, AIs are brilliant at reading long documents›
  • Here's the EU AI Act: 144 pages of regulation›
  • “Can you please summarise the key principles and the risk-tier framework for me?”›
  • A credible summary in seconds›
What AI is good at

Then push back and ask follow-ups.

  • Follow-up questions narrow the gap between what it understood and what you meant›
  • It can correct your framing: “the binding rules are in UK GDPR, not this Act”›
  • A plan you could take to your data protection lead tomorrow, in a fraction of the time›
What AI is good at

Finding structure in messy text.

  • Until recently, pulling facts out of letters, emails and notes meant a person reading and re-typing them›
  • A lot of work is exactly this: read it, then key the right facts into a system›
  • AI is now very good at this. You can treat text as if it were already structured›
What AI is good at

Once it's structured, systems can act on it.

  • Once the facts are structured, other systems can act on them automatically›
  • Log it on the applications register. Acknowledge the applicant›
  • All of this could run with no human input›
What AI is good at

Some actions are expensive if wrong.

  • Approving a bay and ordering road markings costs money, and a resident's trust, if it's wrong›
  • These should go through a human checkpoint. Even a one-click approval is enough›
  • We should keep a human in the loop for important things›
What AI is good at

The limitations so far.

  • Bias›
  • System design›
  • There's one other huge one›
  • Sometimes the AI just makes stuff up. We call that a hallucination›
Hallucinations

A story from a flight.

  • A book opened with a Shakespeare quote, and I wanted some context›
  • No internet, so a small AI on my phone (Llama 3.2)›
  • It claims it did some research. How?›
  • I told it the source: Maria, in Twelfth Night›
  • It names the wrong character. The explanation is wrong too›
Hallucinations

Why does it hallucinate?

  • It doesn't actually know anything›
  • It has learned statistically how to say the best next word, but there's always a chance of the wrong one›
  • Generate for long enough and, at some point, it will say the wrong word›
Hallucinations

Bigger AIs hallucinate less. But how do we know?

  • ChatGPT and Claude answer correctly›
  • But how do we know?›
  • If it's not important: ask again a different way›
  • If it actually matters: verify. Switch on search and check the sources›
Hallucinations

Use your own judgement.

  • Checking takes work, so judge when it matters›
  • A knowledgeable pal in the pub gives you a good general picture. That's fine for lots of things›
  • Presenting to colleagues, or writing to a resident? Check everything›
  • The new skill is knowing when to check, and how hard›
  • And it is quicker to verify than to create›
Hallucinations

Knowledge cutoffs.

  • Trained on massive datasets (the internet plus private data)›
  • They only ‘know’ what that data shows, up to a date›
  • Providers publish each model's cutoff›
  • Think of them as language processors more than databases›
Knowledge cutoffs

To sum up.

  • Modern AIs are very powerful, and flexible›
  • But we are responsible for their outputs and how we use them›
  • Limitations: bias · hallucinations · system design · knowledge cutoffs›
  • Knowing when AI needs checking, and how thoroughly, is a skill we have to develop›
